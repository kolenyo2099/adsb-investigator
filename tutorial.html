ADS-B Investigator

A field guide for reporters, researchers, and anyone else who wants to see where an aircraft has flown — and where the record goes dark.

Contents 1. The core idea (read this first) 2. Looking up one aircraft 3. Reading a gap report 4. Finding aircraft in a region 5. Digging deeper with the Analyze tab 6. What this tool can't tell you 7. Frequently asked questions

1. The core idea (read this first)

This tool reads ADS-B data — the position reports aircraft broadcast in flight, picked up by a global network of volunteer ground receivers. It is not radar, and it is not a flight plan. Two things follow from that, and everything else in this guide builds on them:

There is no "destination" in the data. An aircraft's ADS-B signal simply stops when it flies out of range of every receiver, and starts again when it comes back into range — even if it never landed. This tool never guesses where a plane went during a gap. It shows you the gap, where the aircraft was last seen, and the outer limit of how far it could plausibly have travelled before reappearing.
No signal doesn't mean no flight. Ground-receiver coverage is dense over North America, Europe, and much of Asia — and thin to nonexistent across large parts of Africa, the open ocean, and other sparsely populated regions. A long gap over a place with no coverage tells you nothing was watching, not that nothing happened.

Everything the tool shows you is either a directly observed fact (a position, an altitude, a timestamp) or an honestly-labelled inference with its assumptions stated (the reachable-distance circles in a gap report). If you ever see the tool imply a specific destination — that's a bug, not a feature. Please report it.

2. Looking up one aircraft

The Aircraft trace tab is the starting point. It shows the flight path of one tail number on one day.

1
Type a tail number (like N703KW) or a six-character ICAO hex code (like a960a9) into the first box. Any aircraft works — there's no preset list to pick from.
2
Choose a date and click Look up. If nothing comes back, that means the aircraft either didn't fly that day or flew entirely outside receiver coverage — the app will say so rather than showing you an empty map.
3
The map draws the flight path: a green dot for the first position seen, a black dot for the last, and a blue line connecting every point between.
Why it also loads the two previous days. The underlying data is filed one calendar day at a time, so a flight that crosses midnight would otherwise appear to be two unrelated fragments — and a gap spanning midnight would be invisible entirely. The app quietly stitches the surrounding days together so long gaps are measured correctly.

Below the controls, the sidebar lists every coverage gap in the track — any stretch of 20 minutes or more with no position reports. See the next section for how to read those.

There's also a collapsed "browse archived aircraft" list, which holds aircraft whose data has been saved into the project directly. It's a convenience shortcut, not a limit on what you can search.

3. Reading a gap report

Not all gaps mean the same thing, so the app sorts them into two kinds — and this distinction is the single most useful thing on the screen.

Greyed out — "parked"Red — "left coverage while still airborne"
The track ended at ground level. The aircraft landed and switched off. Completely routine — most gaps are these. The track ended at altitude. The aircraft flew out of receiver range while still flying, and reappeared somewhere else later. This is the kind worth investigating.

Only airborne gaps get a reachable envelope and a circle on the map — drawing one around a parked aircraft would imply a journey that plainly didn't happen.

An airborne gap card shows how long the aircraft was unseen, where it was last observed (position and altitude), and a reachable envelope — how far it could have travelled in that time under a few different assumptions.

1431 min gap from (32.66, -92.87) @ 19075 ft
Reachable envelope: 0h stop → 10137 km · 0.5h stop → 9924 km
                      1h stop → 9712 km · 2h stop → 9287 km

Read that as: the aircraft was last seen at this position and altitude, then vanished from ADS-B coverage for 1,431 minutes (23.8 hours). If it flew the whole time at typical cruise speed, it could be up to 10,137 km from that point. If it also sat on the ground somewhere for up to two hours mid-gap, that shrinks to as little as 9,287 km. The true answer is somewhere in that range — the tool won't pick a point for you, and neither should you, without other evidence.

Use the envelope to rule places out, not in. The most reliable way to use a gap report is negatively: a reachable radius of 700 km rules out a city 2,000 km away. It does not, by itself, confirm the aircraft visited any specific place within the circle.

On the map, each gap is drawn as a dashed red circle centered on the last known position, sized to the largest (most generous) radius in its envelope.

4. Finding aircraft in a region

The Area scan tab answers a different question: not "where did this one aircraft go," but "what aircraft were seen in this region on this day, at all." This is how new leads get found — for instance, narrowing a wide search area down to a handful of candidate aircraft before digging into each one with the Aircraft trace tab.

Area scans are precomputed and only cover the regions and dates someone has already run a scan for — the list in the dropdown is everything currently available. If the region or date you need isn't there, ask whoever maintains this tool's data to run one (see §7).

Each result on the map is a dot for one aircraft, colored by what's known about its registration: red for aircraft registered in the United States (identifiable for free from the aircraft's ICAO identifier), grey for aircraft whose registration country hasn't been resolved yet. The list in the sidebar gives the same information as text, plus the exact position, altitude, and which hour of the day each aircraft was first seen in the scan.

5. Digging deeper with the Analyze tab

If dropdowns and gap cards aren't enough — say, you want to filter, sort, or chart the data yourself — the Analyze tab gives you a real Python data-analysis environment, running entirely in your browser. No installation, no account, and nothing you type or load ever leaves your computer.

1
First, load something to analyze: view an aircraft trace or an area scan in the other tabs. Whatever you looked at last is what gets loaded.
2
Switch to the Analyze tab and click Load Python. This downloads a small Python environment (about 10 MB) — it only happens once per visit, and only if you ask for it.
3
Once it's ready, your data is sitting in a variable called df — standard for a "DataFrame" in the pandas library, which is the standard tool for this kind of data work in Python. Type an expression in the box and click Run.

A few starting points, if Python is new to you:

Type thisTo see
df.head()The first few rows, so you can see what columns exist
df.describe()Quick statistics — min, max, average — for every numeric column
df.sort_values('alt')Every row sorted by altitude, lowest first
df[df['gs'] < 50]Only the rows where ground speed is under 50 knots — useful for spotting where a plane slowed down or was taxiing
len(df)How many rows (position reports, or aircraft) are in the data

A trace's columns are t (timestamp), lat, lon, alt (feet), and gs (ground speed in knots). An area scan's columns include hex, reg_country, lat, lon, alt_ft, and gs_kt.

6. What this tool can't tell you

  • Destination. Never shown, never inferred. See §1.
  • Passengers, cargo, or purpose of flight. ADS-B carries only position and identification data — nothing about what or who is aboard.
  • Certainty during a gap. The reachable-envelope circles are physics, not evidence — they describe what's possible, not what happened.
  • Aircraft not on the watchlist, outside a scanned area, or flying without ADS-B. Some aircraft — often military or state aircraft — don't broadcast at all, or broadcast without a public identifier. Their absence from this tool proves nothing either way.

7. Frequently asked questions

How far back can I search?

The underlying archive keeps roughly the last month of the current year, plus complete past years. That means there can be a hole between the two — for recent months of the current year, data may not be retrievable on demand even though the flight happened. If a date in that window matters to your story, ask the maintainer: it's recoverable from a bulk archive, but not instantly.

I searched a tail number and got nothing. Does that mean it didn't fly?

No — and this is the most important misreading to avoid. It means no receiver logged it. The aircraft may have flown somewhere with no coverage, flown without broadcasting, or not flown at all. The three look identical from here. Check a date you know it flew to confirm the tail number resolves correctly before drawing any conclusion from an empty result.

Can I get area coverage for a new region or date?

Area scans take real time and bandwidth to generate, so they're run on request rather than for the whole world continuously. Ask the maintainer for the date range and bounding box you need.

Is this data authoritative?

It's sourced from a public, volunteer-run ADS-B receiver network, released under a public-domain (CC0) license. Treat it the way you'd treat any single source: strong for what it directly shows (a plane was here, at this time, at this altitude), and it should be corroborated, like any single source, before it anchors a claim on its own.

Why can't the app just show live flights?

By design, this tool only ever shows historical data that's already been fetched and saved — not a live feed. That keeps it lightweight, reproducible, and gentle on the volunteer infrastructure the underlying data comes from.
