Reading the flight record, and its silences

A guide for reporters and researchers who want to know where an aircraft has been, and what it means when the record goes quiet.

1. What this data is, and is not

Aircraft broadcast their position continuously, and a global network of volunteers runs the ground receivers that listen. This tool reads that record. It is not radar, and it carries no flight plan. Two consequences follow, and everything else here rests on them.

The first thing to understand

The data contains no destination. An aircraft's signal stops when it flies beyond the last receiver that can hear it, and resumes when it comes back within range. It may never have landed in between. This tool will not guess where a flight went. It shows you the silence, where the aircraft was last heard, and the furthest it could plausibly have travelled before reappearing.

The second thing to understand

Silence is not absence. Receiver coverage is dense across North America, Europe and much of Asia. It thins to nothing over large parts of Africa, the open oceans, and other sparsely populated regions. A long silence above a place with no receivers tells you only that nobody was listening.

Everything on screen is either something a receiver directly observed, such as a position, an altitude or a timestamp, or a clearly labelled inference with its assumptions written next to it. If the tool ever appears to name a destination, that is a defect worth reporting.

2. Following one aircraft

The Trace tab shows one aircraft's path across one day.

  1. Type a tail number such as N703KW, or a six-character ICAO hex code such as a960a9. Any aircraft works. There is no preset list.

  2. Pick a date and select Look up aircraft. If nothing comes back, the aircraft either did not fly or flew where no receiver could hear it. The tool says so rather than showing an empty map.

  3. The map draws the path: a green dot where it was first heard, a pale dot where it was last heard, and a line through every position between.

Why it loads three days

The archive files one calendar day at a time. A flight crossing midnight would otherwise appear as two unrelated fragments, and a silence spanning midnight would vanish entirely. The tool quietly stitches the surrounding days together so long gaps are measured correctly.

3. Reading a gap report

Gaps are not all alike, and telling them apart is the most useful thing on the screen.

Dimmed, marked "parked" Red, marked "unobserved"
The track ended at ground level. The aircraft landed and switched off. Entirely routine, and most gaps are these. The track ended at altitude. The aircraft flew out of range while still flying, and surfaced somewhere else later. This is the kind worth your attention.

Only unobserved gaps get a reachable envelope and a circle on the map. Drawing one around a parked aircraft would imply a journey that plainly did not happen.

5h 30m  UNOBSERVED
Left coverage at 35,000 ft, over 37.2810, -70.2660

REACHABLE BEFORE IT REAPPEARED
No ground stop                            2,334 km
30 min stop                               2,122 km
1 h stop                                  1,909 km
2 h stop                                  1,484 km

Read that as: the aircraft was last heard at that position and altitude, then went unheard for five and a half hours. Flying the whole time at typical cruise speed, it could be anywhere within 2,334 km of that point. Had it also sat on the ground for two hours, the reachable area shrinks to 1,484 km. The truth lies somewhere in that range, and the tool will not pick a point inside it for you.

How to use the envelope honestly

It rules places out, not in. A reachable radius of 700 km excludes a city 2,000 km away, and that exclusion is solid. It never confirms the aircraft visited any particular place inside the circle. Treat the envelope as a boundary on the possible, not as evidence of a route.

4. Where a flight began and ended

Under each aircraft you will find two cards describing the ends of the track. They are the closest this tool comes to naming an origin and a destination, and the distinction they draw is deliberate.

Observed, not inferred

Green means the aircraft was actually on the ground there. The track begins or ends at ground level, so a receiver watched it sitting on the surface, and the nearest airfield within 6 km is named. That is an observation, not a guess.

The other case

Grey means the track begins or ends in the air. All you know is where the aircraft entered or left receiver range. It was flying before, and it kept flying after. The card reports the altitude, whether it was climbing or descending, and the nearest airfield as context only.

That context line is where your judgement comes in, and it is worth an example. A track that ends at 425 ft, three kilometres from a runway, still reads destination not observed. That is technically correct: nobody watched it touch down, and it could have gone around. But an aircraft descending through 425 ft that close to a runway is landing, and any reader can draw that conclusion. The tool gives you the altitude, the trend and the distance, and leaves the inference to you rather than making it silently.

The same aircraft's first card might read first heard airborne at 9,625 ft, climbing, 8 km from Alexandria International. Again, it almost certainly departed there, and again the tool declines to write that down for you. The rule throughout is that observations and inferences never share a sentence.

On the map, the same distinction is drawn as filled dots for positions observed on the ground and hollow dots where a track merely entered or left coverage.

5. Finding aircraft over a region

The Area tab answers a different question: not where one aircraft went, but which aircraft were seen over a region on a given day. This is how new leads surface, by narrowing a wide search down to a few candidates worth examining one by one.

  1. Choose an indexed day. These are days the nightly job has already summarised.

  2. Pan and zoom the map until it frames the region you care about.

  3. Narrow with the filters below, then select Search this map view. Filtering runs on your own machine and returns immediately.

  4. Select any result to pull that aircraft's full track for the day, which switches you to the Trace tab with everything filled in.

The filters, and what each one is for

A busy region can return thousands of aircraft, nearly all of them airliners crossing overhead. These filters exist to strip that away.

FilterWhat it finds
Presence in view Set to stayed entirely in the view, this drops every aircraft that was merely passing through, leaving only those whose whole day happened inside your frame. Usually the single biggest cut.
Ceiling The highest altitude an aircraft reached that day. A low maximum excludes cruising airliners and leaves local flying.
Active from / to The hours during which it was heard. Useful for isolating activity at night, or around a known event.
Max range How far apart the furthest two points of its day were. A small figure means the aircraft circled rather than travelled, which is the signature of survey, training and surveillance patterns.
Registration Whether the ICAO address falls in the United States range. Exact and free, unlike anything about the route.

Combining a low ceiling with a short range over a single region is an effective way to surface aircraft that spent the day orbiting rather than going anywhere.

Drawing the flight paths

Once a search is down to a few hundred aircraft, Draw flight paths replaces the dots with the routes themselves. The paths come from the same nightly index, so this is one download for the whole day rather than a request per aircraft.

The shapes are the point. Rectangular traffic circuits, holding orbits and survey grids are unmistakable as lines and completely invisible as dots. Hover a path for its ceiling, hours and position count; select one to identify the aircraft and open its full record.

What simplification means here

Paths are thinned in proportion to how far each aircraft ranged that day, so a tight circuit keeps its corners while a long crossing is reduced to the turns that matter. The shape is faithful; the fine detail between turns is not. For exact positions, open the aircraft's full record.

The legend

The panel at the bottom left of the map explains the current view and changes with the tab you are on. It can be collapsed once the symbols are familiar. The distinction worth remembering is the one in section 4: filled dots mark positions observed on the ground, hollow dots mark where a track merely entered or left receiver coverage.

Results are coloured by what the ICAO address reveals about registration: red for United States registration, grey where the country has not been resolved. The registration country is exact and free, read straight from the aircraft's address, unlike anything about its route.

6. Going further with Python

When the built-in views run out, the Analyze tab gives you a full Python data environment running inside your browser. Nothing is installed, no account is needed, and nothing you load or type is uploaded anywhere.

  1. View a trace or run an area search first. Whatever you looked at last is what gets loaded.

  2. Select Load Python. About 10 MB downloads once per visit, and only when you ask for it.

  3. Your data waits in a variable called df, a pandas DataFrame. Type an expression and select Run.

ExpressionWhat it shows
df.head()The first rows, so you can see which columns exist
df.describe()Minimum, maximum and average for every numeric column
df.sort_values('alt')Every row by altitude, lowest first
df[df['gs'] < 50]Rows under 50 knots, useful for spotting where an aircraft slowed or taxied
len(df)How many rows you are working with

A trace carries t, lat, lon, alt and gs. An area search carries hex, reg_country, the four corners of where each aircraft was seen, max_alt_ft and positions.

7. What this tool cannot tell you

  • Where a flight was going. Never shown, never inferred.
  • Who or what was aboard. The broadcast carries position and identity, and nothing about passengers or cargo.
  • What happened during a silence. The envelope is physics rather than evidence. It describes what was possible.
  • Anything about aircraft that do not broadcast. Some aircraft, often military or state operated, transmit nothing at all or transmit without a public identity. Their absence here proves nothing in either direction.

8. Questions

How far back can I search?

The upstream archive keeps roughly the last month of the current year plus complete past years, which can leave a hole between the two. For recent months of this year, a flight may be unreachable on demand even though it happened. If such a date matters to your story, ask whoever maintains this tool. The data is recoverable from bulk archives, though not instantly.

I searched a tail number and found nothing. Did it not fly?

Unknown, and this is the most important misreading to avoid. An empty result means no receiver logged the aircraft. It may have flown beyond coverage, flown without broadcasting, or not flown at all. All three look identical from here. Before concluding anything, check a date you know it flew, to confirm the tail number resolves correctly.

Is this data authoritative?

It comes from a public, volunteer-run receiver network and is released into the public domain. Treat it as you would any single source: strong evidence for what it directly records, and worth corroborating before it carries a claim on its own.

Why does it not show live flights?

By design it reads only historical records that have already been published. That keeps the tool light, keeps results reproducible months later, and stays gentle on the volunteer infrastructure the data comes from.
